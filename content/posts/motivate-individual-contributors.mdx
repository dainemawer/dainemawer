Individual contributors carry a lot of the actual weight in a team, and an engineering manager's job is largely about leading them toward good outcomes while keeping the work sustainable. Most managers were individual contributors themselves not long ago, which helps: it's easier to empathize with a role you've actually done.

Everyone brings a different personality to the table, so motivating people well means adjusting your approach as workload and pressure shift, not running the same playbook on everyone. A few things consistently help.

## Set clear goals and expectations

People need to know what's expected of them. It gives them a sense of security and a reason for being there, and it's the starting point for understanding how their work fits into the team's success.

SMART goals, specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound, are a reliable way to frame this so contributors know what "done well" actually looks like.

Project goals matter, but personal ones matter just as much. Individual contributors usually want to learn things beyond whatever the current stack happens to be, and it's worth remembering that as a manager. Setting yearly personal goals alongside team ones helps someone feel like they're building their own career, not just delivering the roadmap.

## Give ongoing feedback and support

Continuous, genuinely positive feedback is what actually helps someone thrive, more than any single review cycle.

That can happen daily, weekly, or monthly, through:

- Async check-ins on Slack
- 1:1 meetings
- Quarterly goal realignments

Rapport makes this easier. Ongoing support helps someone see their own strengths and weaknesses more clearly, and gives a manager a natural way into mentorship when it's needed, including [the version of it that happens in a pull request](/effective-code-reviews).

The part that matters most is following through. Don't promise to help someone hit a goal and then not mention it again for six months. Trust is the whole point here, and it erodes fast if it's not backed up. When things get hard, staying calm and steady matters more than having the perfect answer.

Some days, support has nothing to do with goals or output at all. Sometimes it's just someone having a bad day, and what they need is empathy, not a plan.

## Support career development, even beyond this job

An individual contributor's career goals won't always line up with the company's. That's normal, and worth respecting rather than fighting. Taking a genuine interest in where someone wants to go tends to motivate them more than almost anything else on this list.

A few things that help in practice:

- Professional development budgets
- Sponsored conferences and trips
- Encouraging upskilling that benefits both the person and the company
- Giving people responsibility on projects that actually build the skills they want

Conversations about a career path that leads outside the company can feel awkward, but most individual contributors will move on eventually. The time they spend on your team should leave them better prepared for whatever comes next, not just useful to you while they're there. That's harder and more important for early-career engineers now that [the work they used to learn on is the work agents do fastest](/keeping-the-junior-pipeline-alive).

## Recognize and reward contributions

Recognition moves morale more than reward does. Public recognition, in front of peers or non-technical stakeholders, carries real weight. And it shouldn't be reserved for good code alone: strong teamwork, going above and beyond, or a genuine effort to improve all deserve the same treatment.

Someone putting in that extra effort is usually a sign they care about the role, and that's worth acknowledging on its own terms.

## Build a positive, inclusive environment

Keep hard conversations honest but inclusive. Transparent communication across the team, regardless of seniority, helps people feel respected and included in decisions that affect them, technical or otherwise. It closes distance between people faster than almost anything else.

Make space for mistakes people can actually learn from, and take feedback seriously when it comes back to you. A team that feels genuinely close-knit is one people want to show up for, not just one they're obligated to.

That's the start of a real culture, not a stated one, and it's usually what separates a good team from a great one.
